Parts key

D-head: flat right-angle hex driver head (develop first)

Power base: shared across the family (phase 2; phase 1 uses any 1/4 in driver)

H-head: angled linear striker (paused)

Work pieces

Engineering verdict

Develop the driver head and pause the hammer head. Build the driver head as a passive attachment for drivers people already own. Commit to a shared power base only after the attachment proves people want it.

The D-head turns a 4 mm hex bolt 180 mm deep inside a 40 mm void, where the $48 right-angle adapter cannot enter. The mechanism is ordinary: a shaft, a 2.6:1 crown gear and a through-bore bit. The business case is weak, because most people meet J1 rarely and can tip the dresser over instead.

Mechanical feasibility
Likely

Stack 25 mm calc fits the 40 mm void measure, with 8.5 mm to spare while sliding in. Crown-gear tooth bending at 8 N·m is about 373 MPa (Lewis, module 1, 5 mm face) calc, which needs case-hardened steel.

Usable by one person
Yes, two-handed

Main hand on the driver, second hand on the sleeve pushing about 39 N against the reaction torque calc. The bolt takes 9.4 s at 154 rpm, against an estimated 2–3 min with an L-key estimate.

Commercial promise
Weak

It must sell near the $46–50 Milwaukee adapter and the $20–49 DeWalt adapter. It only wins in voids between 35.5 and about 57 mm. Landed cost at 1,000 units is $20.52–$36.96 estimate.

Unknown most likely to change the verdict: how often awkward fasteners sit in voids between 35.5 and 57 mm. If real voids are mostly 57 mm or more, the cheap adapter wins. If they are mostly under 35.5 mm, the D-head needs custom short bits and a thinner gear.

What to share across the family. Keep the heads mechanically passive and share the battery, controller, motor, gearbox and one bayonet coupling with a 1/4 in hex output (B1–B8). Engineering points this way for three reasons:

  • A passive head costs $20.52–$36.96 to land, while a head with its own motor costs about 3–4× that estimate.
  • Only the base needs UL 62841 and battery work.
  • Bosch FlexiClick and Milwaukee 2505 already prove that buyers accept swapping heads on a shared base.

The bend angle is carried by the grip hinge (B6), which passes only wires, so no universal joint sits in the drivetrain. A variable-bend head would need a U-joint that adds at least 15 mm to the stack estimate, so the D-head stays at 90° and rotates in 12 steps of 30° about its input axis (D2).

Where the hammer head fits. The H-head would plug into the same bayonet (B7). Its cam-spring striker would be driven from the 1/4 in output.

  • A palm nailer delivers 3 J × 2,700 bpm, about 135 W of blow power calc from published specs. That is within reach of a 12 V base.
  • Recoil then runs back through the bayonet and planetary gearbox. That needs a damper, and is a durability risk the D-head never puts on the base.

"Swing at an angle" means one of two mechanisms, compared for job J2: driving a 76 mm hanger nail with 150 mm clear along the nail axis measure.

  • Pivoting striking face. A hammer face on a short arm swings about a pin inside the head. The face is square to the nail at only one point of its arc. As the nail sinks, the blow turns glancing, and the head has no surface that registers on the nail. Recoil loads the pivot pin on every blow.
  • Linear striker in an adjustable head. A striker moves along the nail axis inside a nose that sits on the nail head, so every blow is coaxial. The angle adjustment (H3) is between head and handle, outside the impact line, but it still carries recoil to the handle, so it needs locking teeth.

The linear striker wins. It needs about 146 mm at the start of a drive calc. The Bostitch PN50 needs 183 mm and the Milwaukee 2458-20 241 mm calc. So the H-head only helps in bays between about 146 and 183 mm, and only where a hand cannot sit behind the nail. Palm nailers already handle the common case for $55–203. Pause the H-head.

Alternatives

The closest direct products are modular 12 V drivers with right-angle heads and powered ratchets. The workarounds people actually use are cheap adapters, L-keys and tipping the furniture over. The strongest reason to keep the existing solution: J1 is a once-in-a-while job, and an L-key or tipping the dresser is free.

Partners and standards

  1. First: a freelance mechanical engineer with gear or power-tool experience. Ask for a 2-page gear sizing and tolerance memo (module, face width, material, heat treatment, backlash), with printable CAD for the stage 1 head. Budget 15–25 h at about $75–130/h (rate guide).
  2. Second: a CNC shop through an instant-quote service for the stage 2 housings and gears (Xometry).
  3. Can wait until stage 0 passes: a patent attorney for a freedom-to-operate search (right-angle and offset heads are crowded), an injection moulder, and an NRTL lab. A lab is needed only if you build the power base.

Parts key

P-1 lift platform

Work piece

Engineering verdict

Narrow before building. A powered thin platform can lift and steer a 60 kg chest on hard floors. It cannot slide under items with less than 61 mm of underside clearance, cannot cross sills over about 2.1 mm without a ramp, and does not work on carpet.

Inside those limits the hard part is not the machine. The hard part is whether people living alone fail at the lift step of a $29 lever kit, or at the push step. That decides whether this should be a lift-only aid, a full mover, or nothing.

Mechanical feasibility
Feasible, narrow

Toggle links lift 49.5 mm from a 45 mm tray target. The lead screw needs 1.45 N·m at deck contact calc, against a 2.45 N·m motor limit sourced. With Ø38 mm wheels and 1 in ball transfers, sills over 2.1 mm stall the drive calc.

Usable by one person
Conditional

No lifting by the user and hold-to-run steering. But the item must have legs (61 mm or more clearance), the user pushes the platform in with a handle, and placing a ramp at each sill is a bend-down task. Flush-bottom items need a second person or a lever accessory.

Commercial promise
Weak to unproven

Landed cost is $254.88–$436.80 at 1,000 units estimate, implying a $650–900 retail price, against a $29 lever kit and $14 sliders. No consumer powered competitor was found, and Airsled sells a thin powered mover to pros at $530–840.

Unknown most likely to change the verdict: which step of a manual move target users cannot do: tipping the item to insert sliders, pushing it, or crossing sills. Stage 0 measures this directly with a $90 kit.

Alternatives

The closest direct products are manual lift-and-roll kits and pro movers. No consumer powered under-furniture mover turned up. The strongest reason to keep the existing solution: a $29 lever kit plus sliders moves the same chest, works on carpet, and needs no charging.

Partners and standards

  1. First: an occupational therapist or a Certified Aging-in-Place Specialist. Ask for a one-page session protocol with force and posture stop limits, and help recruiting 3 users. Deliverable: observation notes per step. Budget 6–10 h estimate.
  2. Second, only if stage 0 passes: a mechatronics contractor (ME and EE). Ask for link and screw sizing, a motor-control and hold-to-run architecture, and a hazard list (pinch, tip, runaway, stall).
  3. Then: an NRTL scoping call to settle UL 60335-1 alone versus also UL 3300, and a product-liability insurance broker. Both can wait until the integrated prototype works.

Provisional choices and which ones change the design
  • Changes design
    Item: emptied 4-drawer chest, 60 kg, 1100 × 480 × 1000 mm, CG 450 mm up and centred
    Mass sets motor sizing. CG height and depth set the tipping margin (0.40 g across the platform).
  • Changes design
    Underside clearance 76 mm (3 in, the low end of published dresser specs)
    Sets the stowed height. Below 61 mm the lift motor stalls; below 55 mm the platform does not fit at all.
  • Changes design
    Platform 700 × 360 mm, stowed 45 mm, lift 49.5 mm
    A larger footprint raises the tip margin but stops it fitting between feet.
  • Changes design
    Hard floors only; ramps for sills over about 2 mm
    Carpet roughly triples rolling resistance (CasterHQ table) and the ball transfers sink in. Carpet needs larger wheels and therefore a taller tray.
  • Changes design
    Route: 914 mm (36 in) hallway into a 737 mm clear (32 in) door
    Narrower doors stop longer items, whatever the mover.
  • Minor
    Speed 0.3 m/s, programmed stops at 0.1 g, wired hold-to-run pendant
    Safety defaults; easy to change in firmware.
  • Minor
    One lift motor with a left/right-hand screw
    Keeps the deck level. Two motors would allow tilting but add cost.
