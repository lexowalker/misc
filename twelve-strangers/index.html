Reporting

IBefore history to c. 1900 BCE

The Circle

Long before courts, the people who judged you were the people who knew you. All of them.

For almost all of human history, people lived in groups small enough that everyone knew everyone. The anthropologist Robin Dunbar argues that the human brain tops out at about 150 stable relationships, roughly the size of a forager clan or a traditional village. Other researchers dispute the exact number, but not the scale. Inside a group that size there is no need to ask whether the judges know the accused. They all do.

Speculation · not in the record

Nobody wrote down how the first humans settled a quarrel. But anthropologists who lived with small communities in the last two centuries saw a common pattern, and it is a fair guess that it is very old. The whole group listens. Kin speak up for kin. Old grudges come out. Your reputation is the main evidence. And because everyone has to go on living together, the goal is usually repair rather than punishment: give back what was taken, pay something, make peace.

Some of these gatherings were studied up close. Among the Kpelle of Liberia, the anthropologist James Gibbs described the house palaver, a meeting of kin and neighbors held in the home of the complainant, where everyone could speak and the aim was to send both sides home reconciled. Gibbs called it therapy as much as law.

The first case on clay

The oldest trial record of this kind comes from Mesopotamia. Around 1900 BCE, in the city of Nippur, three men killed a priest named Lu-Inanna and then told his wife, Nin-dada. She said nothing. The king sent the case to the Assembly of Nippur, a gathering of citizens. Nine men spoke for putting her to death along with the killers. Two spoke for her: she had not killed anyone. In the reading of the scholar Thorkild Jacobsen, the Assembly decided that a wife who keeps silent about her husband's murder shares the guilt, and all four were condemned. An earlier translator read the tablet the other way and had her set free, which tells you how hard four-thousand-year-old law can be to read.

The judges were not professionals. They were the city's own people, judging a crime that had happened among them. That idea would keep coming back.

IIAthens, 508 to 322 BCE

Justice by Lottery

Athens handed its courts to hundreds of ordinary citizens at a time, picked at random so that nobody could buy them.

Democratic Athens had no professional judges and no public prosecutor. A citizen brought the charge, both sides spoke for themselves against a water clock, and a huge panel of citizens voted straight away. There was no deliberation and no discussion. Each juror dropped a ballot, and the majority won.

Every year 6,000 citizens over the age of thirty, roughly one adult male citizen in five, swore the jurors' oath. On a court day they gathered at dawn, and a stone machine decided which of them would serve.

Why so many jurors? A panel of 501 is hard to bribe and hard to frighten. Why chosen at random, on the morning of the trial? Because if nobody knows in advance who will judge, nobody can buy the judges. Athens fought corruption with chance, the same tool a modern court uses when it draws names from a voter list. It had reason to. Aristotle names the first man to bribe an Athenian jury: Anytus, in 409 BCE. Ten years later the same Anytus helped prosecute Socrates.

Jurors were paid, three obols a day from the 420s BCE, which drew the old and the poor. The comic playwright Aristophanes mocked them in Wasps (422 BCE) as cranky old men addicted to the thrill of voting.

Were they your peers? In one sense, very much. In a city of perhaps 30,000 adult male citizens, 501 jurors were a real slice of the citizen body. In another sense, not at all. Women, enslaved people and resident foreigners could never sit. And Athens was already too big for everyone to know everyone. By design, most of the men voting on your fate had never met you. The twelve-strangers problem is at least 2,400 years old.

Meanwhile in Rome

Rome tried serious crimes in standing courts, the quaestiones perpetuae, before large panels of jurors drawn from an official list, the album iudicum. Who got onto that list was a political brawl. In 122 BCE Gaius Gracchus moved the seats from senators to the wealthy order of knights. Sulla gave them back to the Senate. In 70 BCE a compromise split them among senators, knights and a class of treasury officials. Rome was arguing about a question that never went away: who counts as a fit judge of a citizen?

Neither Athens nor Rome is a direct ancestor of the English jury. Historians see no line of descent. They are better read as proof that people keep reinventing the idea of ordinary citizens judging each other.

IIINorthern Europe, 500 to 1215

Oath, Ordeal or Battle

In early medieval Europe your neighbors vouched for you, and when they could not settle it, God would.

Across northern Europe, free men met in open-air assemblies called things. The Roman historian Tacitus, writing in 98 CE, described Germanic assemblies that heard capital charges and showed approval by brandishing their spears. In Iceland, the Althing, founded around 930, ran a whole country with no king. Its Lawspeaker recited the law from memory, one third of it each summer. In the Viking-settled parts of England, a district was a wapentake, from the Old Norse for “weapon-taking,” the clash of arms that signaled yes.

Your word, and your neighbors' word

One way to clear yourself was compurgation. You swore you were innocent, then brought oath-helpers who swore that your oath was clean. They were not witnesses. They were character references with their souls on the line. A well-known local could clear himself with ease. A stranger with nobody to swear for him was in trouble.

Around 997 King Æthelred II ordered that in each wapentake the twelve leading thegns, with the reeve, should swear on holy relics to accuse no innocent man and shield no guilty one. If they split, the view of eight of them stood, and each outvoted thegn paid a fine. Historians still argue whether these twelve are the jury's true ancestor. They are certainly England's first famous twelve.

The Normans brought another tool from France: the inquest. A royal official summoned local men, put them on oath and made them tell the truth about the king's lands and rights. In 1086 William the Conqueror's commissioners used sworn local juries, hundred by hundred, to compile Domesday Book: who held each place before the Conquest, who held it now, and what it was worth.

When the neighbors could not decide

Then came the ordeal, a church ritual. In the ordeal of hot iron you carried a blessed red-hot iron nine of your own feet. Your hand was bound and sealed, and three days later a clean wound meant innocence and a festering one meant guilt. In the ordeal of cold water you were lowered on a rope into blessed water. If you sank, the water had accepted you. You were innocent, and they pulled you out. The Normans added trial by battle: God would give victory to the side in the right.

The claimOrdeals were rigged so that almost everyone failed.

Sustained

Where records survive, most people passed. In a church register from Várad in Hungary (1208 to 1235), 130 of the 208 people who actually carried the hot iron were cleared. In 1098 fifty Englishmen accused of poaching the king's deer all passed the hot iron, and King William Rufus scoffed at the whole idea. The economist Peter Leeson argues ordeals worked because people believed in them: the guilty tended to confess or settle, and priests could quietly make the test survivable for those who did go through with it.

The claimTrial by battle was still legal in England in 1818.

Overruled

It is true. In Ashford v. Thornton (1818), a man acquitted of murder answered a private appeal by throwing down a glove in the Court of King's Bench and demanding to fight. The judges agreed the law allowed it. The accuser backed down, and Parliament abolished trial by battle in 1819.

IVEngland, 1166 to 1215

The King's Twelve

A king who wanted order, a pope who took God out of the courtroom, and a clause about barons that grew into a promise for everyone.

Henry II wanted his peace kept, and he used neighbors to keep it. His Assize of Clarendon (1166) ordered twelve lawful men of every hundred, and four of every village, to name on oath anyone suspected of robbery, murder or theft. These twelve were accusers, not judges. The people they named went to the water ordeal, and God decided. Even passing was not always enough: a man the jurors called notorious could be made to leave the realm anyway.

For land, Henry offered something new. In quick actions over who had been thrown off a holding, twelve local men answered a single question about facts they could see for themselves. And from the late 1170s, under the Grand Assize, a landholder challenged over his title could refuse to fight and let twelve knights of the county decide who had the better right.

1215: God leaves the courtroom

In November 1215 the Fourth Lateran Council in Rome forbade priests to bless ordeals. Without a priest, an ordeal was just an injury. English justices suddenly had no way to decide criminal cases. In January 1219 the young Henry III's council wrote to the judges with makeshift advice, and the judges improvised with the sworn neighbors they already had. They began asking a jury not only whom it suspected, but whether he was guilty.

An accused person had to agree to this new trial. In the words of the court rolls, he put himself upon the country, meaning on the verdict of his neighbors.

The judgment of his peers

Earlier in 1215, at Runnymede, rebel barons had forced King John to seal Magna Carta. Its most famous clause promises that no free man will be punished except by the lawful judgment of his peers or by the law of the land. Americans read it as the birth certificate of the jury. Read the Latin closely and it says something narrower.

So the first “jury of your peers” was a club for the rich: barons judged by barons. Peers of the realm kept a version of that privilege for more than 700 years. The last English lord tried by the House of Lords, Lord de Clifford, was acquitted of manslaughter in 1935 after a fatal car crash. Parliament abolished the privilege in 1948.

VEngland, 1215 to 1700

Twelve Who Knew Too Much

For centuries a jury's whole value was what it already knew. Then, slowly, knowing became the problem.

Medieval jurors were summoned from the neighborhood of the crime, the vicinage, precisely because they knew the people, the land and the gossip. Historians call them self-informing. Courts expected them to find things out before they came to court. A jury of strangers would have been useless. It could not tell the judges anything.

Being judged by people who knew you could be a blessing or a terror. A man with a good name had friends in the box. A man with enemies had enemies in the box. And there was no backing out. A defendant who refused to put himself upon the country could be squeezed until he agreed. From 1275 the law allowed prison forte et dure, which became peine forte et dure: pressing under heavy weights. In Salem, Massachusetts, in 1692, Giles Corey refused to plead to a charge of witchcraft and was pressed to death. England ended the practice in 1772. From 1827 a silent defendant was simply treated as pleading not guilty.

Peers, literally

Some medieval juries were built to make “peers” literal. When a foreign merchant was on trial, he could ask for a jury de medietate linguae, “of the half tongue”: half Englishmen and half foreigners. A royal charter for foreign merchants in 1303 and a statute of 1353 formalized it, and England kept it until 1870. When a condemned woman claimed to be pregnant, the court summoned a jury of matrons to examine her. For centuries it was the only jury women could sit on.

From knowing to hearing

Over time the jury's knowledge mattered less and the evidence put before it mattered more. By the 1470s the judge Sir John Fortescue was describing English juries that heard witnesses in open court. Across the 1500s and 1600s, as towns grew and cases got more complicated, the balance tipped from what jurors knew to what they were shown. A neighbor who knew the facts was becoming a witness instead of a juror.

Some things stayed fixed. The verdict had to be unanimous, a rule settled by the late 1300s. Juries that could not agree could be kept without food, drink, fire or light until they did. Jurors had to meet property requirements, so the “country” that judged you meant the men of your county who owned land. And there were nearly always twelve of them. Why twelve?

VILondon and America, 1670 to 1791

The Jury Talks Back

Once jurors stopped being witnesses, they became something new: a wall between the citizen and the state.

A jury that decides on the evidence can also decide against the government. English judges knew it and tried to keep juries in line. In 1554 a London jury acquitted Sir Nicholas Throckmorton of treason against Queen Mary, and the jurors were jailed and heavily fined for it. More than a century later, a jury finally made it stick that jurors may not be punished for their verdict.

Across the Atlantic

The American colonists took the lesson to heart. In New York in 1735 the printer John Peter Zenger was tried for seditious libel against the royal governor. Under the law as the judges stated it, the truth of what he printed was no defense. His lawyer, Andrew Hamilton of Philadelphia, asked the jury to judge both the law and the facts. They acquitted.

When Parliament sent smuggling and tax cases to vice-admiralty courts, where a judge sat without a jury, the colonies treated it as an attack on their liberty. The Declaration of Independence lists among King George's offenses depriving us in many cases, of the benefits of Trial by Jury and transporting us beyond Seas to be tried for pretended offences.

So the new nation wrote the jury in three times over. Article III requires that the Trial of all Crimes, except in Cases of Impeachment, shall be by Jury. The Sixth Amendment promises an accused a speedy and public trial, by an impartial jury of the State and district wherein the crime shall have been committed. The Seventh keeps the civil jury in suits at common law worth more than twenty dollars.

I consider trial by jury as the only anchor ever yet imagined by man, by which a government can be held to the principles of its constitution.

Thomas Jefferson, letter to Thomas Paine, 1789

The claimThe Constitution guarantees you a jury of your peers.

Sustained

The phrase appears nowhere in the Constitution or the Bill of Rights. The Sixth Amendment promises an impartial jury from the state and district of the crime. “Peers” comes from Magna Carta, where it meant equals in rank, and from the English legal writers the founders read.

VIIUnited States, 1791 to 2020

Who Got to Sit

The founders' jury of peers was, in practice, a jury of white men who owned property. Opening the box took two centuries.

Early American jurors were drawn from the men who could vote, and in most places that meant white men with property. Each widening of the jury box came from a fight, usually in the Supreme Court, and many of the formal victories took decades to reach real courtrooms.

Smaller, and then unanimous again

In the twentieth century the Court also asked whether twelve, and unanimity, were essential. In Williams v. Florida (1970) it upheld a six-person jury and called the number twelve a historical accident. In Ballew v. Georgia (1978) it drew the line at five. In Apodaca v. Oregon (1972) it let states convict on a split vote.

That last rule had an ugly history. Louisiana adopted non-unanimous verdicts at its 1898 constitutional convention, a gathering openly devoted to white supremacy, so that a white majority could outvote Black jurors. In Ramos v. Louisiana (2020) the Court overruled Apodaca and held that a state conviction for a serious crime requires a unanimous jury.

VIII1300 against 2026

The Great Inversion

Almost everything that once made you a good juror now gets you sent home.

Put a juror from 1300 next to a juror from 2026 and they look like opposites. One was chosen because he knew. The other is chosen because she does not. Try sorting a few would-be jurors yourself.

What “peers” means now

Watch what happened to the word. In 1215 “peers” meant your equals in rank. By 1300, in practice, it meant your neighbors. Today the Supreme Court treats it as something closer to statistics. In Taylor v. Louisiana (1975) it held that the jury pool must be drawn from a fair cross section of the community. In Holland v. Illinois (1990) it made clear that the guarantee covers the pool, not the twelve who end up in the box.

So you have no right to jurors who look like you, live near you or know anything about you. You have a right not to have whole groups of your community shut out. The modern peer is not someone like you. It is anyone at all, chosen fairly.

IXToday

Twelve Strangers

In a big city, the people who judge you will almost certainly be strangers. That is the design.

Here is how most American juries are made today. The court takes lists of residents, usually registered voters and licensed drivers, and mails summonses at random. Many never arrive and many are ignored, especially in big cities. Those who come fill out a questionnaire and wait. Then the lawyers and the judge question them, in a process called voir dire, and strike anyone who knows the parties, the lawyers or the case, or who cannot be fair. Each side may also strike a few people without giving a reason, as long as the reason is not race or sex.

What survives is close to the opposite of a medieval jury: twelve people, sometimes six or eight, chosen because they have no connection to you. Federal jurors are paid $50 a day.

The math is the whole story. Medieval rules picked jurors who knew you, but in a village of a few hundred even a lottery would have. Today's rules screen out anyone who knows you, but in a city of a million people a lottery almost never finds one anyway. The jury of strangers is a product of scale as much as of law.

Modern courts protect that ignorance hard. Jurors are told not to look anything up, not to visit the scene and not to discuss the case online. In England in 2012 a juror named Theodora Dallas was jailed for six months for searching the internet about a defendant and telling her fellow jurors what she found. Her medieval counterparts would have been expected to do exactly that.

And most cases never reach a jury at all

The biggest change is easy to miss. The jury the founders fought for now decides only a sliver of criminal cases. Most end in a plea bargain, negotiated by lawyers and approved by a judge, with no jury in the room.

XNow and next

Neighbors or Strangers?

Each version of the jury solved one problem and created another.

The jury of neighbors knew the people and the place. It also knew the feuds, feared the local lord and shared the local prejudice. In 1955 an all-white jury in Sumner, Mississippi, acquitted the two men who had murdered fourteen-year-old Emmett Till. They later admitted the killing to a magazine. A jury of the community can be the community at its worst.

The jury of strangers owes nobody anything. It can be fair to an outsider in a way neighbors rarely were. But it knows nothing about you, your street or your life, and it has to learn it all from lawyers in a few days. Studies suggest a mix helps: in a 2006 experiment, the psychologist Samuel Sommers found that racially diverse mock juries deliberated longer, discussed more of the facts and made fewer factual errors than all-white ones.

The jury elsewhere

Most of the world does without a classic jury, and the jury is shrinking where it began. England and Wales have allowed verdicts of ten to two since 1967, and the government has moved to send more cases to judges sitting alone to clear a huge backlog. Scotland, which used fifteen jurors and a third verdict, “not proven,” voted in 2025 to abolish it. India ended jury trials after the sensational Nanavati case of 1959. Other countries are moving the other way: Japan began seating ordinary citizens beside professional judges in 2009, and several Argentine provinces have adopted juries since 2015.

So who are your peers?

Maybe the strangers are your peers after all, in the one sense that has survived every change. They are not your equals in rank, and they are not your neighbors. They are your equals before the law: people summoned by the same lottery, bound by the same rules, and liable to stand where you stand. Alexis de Tocqueville called the American jury a free school, always open, in which every juror learns his rights. In a city of strangers, it may be one of the last places where twelve of them must sit in a room and agree.
