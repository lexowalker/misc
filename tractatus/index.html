Tractatus Logico-Philosophicus

Ludwig Wittgenstein · 1921 · 526 numbered remarks

Tractatus Logico-Philosophicus

A guided expedition to the limit of what can be said.

What can be said at all can be said clearly; and whereof one cannot speak thereof one must be silent.

Wittgenstein, from the preface

Scroll to begin ↓

The book

Ludwig Wittgenstein wrote this book while he was a soldier in the First World War. He completed it in 1918. It appeared in German in 1921 and in English in 1922. It has about 75 pages.

The book has one aim. It tries to find the limit of what language can say. Wittgenstein states the aim in his preface:

The book will, therefore, draw a limit to thinking, or rather—not to thinking, but to the expression of thoughts; for, in order to draw a limit to thinking we should have to be able to think both sides of this limit (we should therefore have to be able to think what cannot be thought).

The limit can, therefore, only be drawn in language and what lies on the other side of the limit will be simply nonsense.

The method is unusual. The book does not argue in chapters. It gives 526 short remarks. Some are one line long. Each remark has a number, and the number gives its place in a strict order.

How the numbers work

Seven remarks have whole numbers, 1 to 7. They are the main propositions. Remark 2.1 is a comment on remark 2. Remark 2.11 is a comment on 2.1. Each added digit goes one level deeper. Wittgenstein explains this in his only footnote to the main text:

Select a number. You see the chain of remarks that it hangs on.

Wittgenstein does not explain the zeros in numbers such as 2.01. Most readers take 2.01 to be a comment on 2 that has less weight than 2.1, or that prepares it.

The map

In this guide the book is also a place. Each remark is one star. Each star is attached to the remark that it comments on. At this moment you see a cloud of points without clear order. As you read, the connections appear, one station after another.

The shows the full map at any time. There you can read all 526 remarks, in English and in German.

Three voices

The guide uses three voices. Each has its own typeface, so that you always know who speaks.

This is Wittgenstein. His text is set like the one above, with his number in the margin. Select a number to see the remark in its place in the book.

This is the guide. It explains the text in plain words. It uses short sentences on purpose. A dotted line marks a technical term. Select the term to see its definition.

This marks an interpretation. Scholars disagree about much of this book. Where the guide goes beyond what the text says, it tells you.

This marks a note on translation. The English text is the first translation, from 1922. It carries the name of C. K. Ogden. Frank Ramsey, then a student, did most of the work, and Wittgenstein corrected it. A later translation (Pears and McGuinness, 1961) uses different English words for some key terms. The guide tells you where this matters.

Station 1 · Remarks 1 – 1.21

1

The book starts with the largest subject there is: everything. Its first step is to say what kind of “everything” the world is.

“What is the case” means: what is so. It rains. The lamp is on the table. Each of these is a fact.

This is the first surprise. We usually think that the world is a very large collection of things: stars, tables, people. Wittgenstein says that this is wrong. A list of things does not give you a world.

Here is an example. A room contains a table, a lamp, and a book. Make a list: table, lamp, book. The list does not tell you if the book is on the table or under it. It does not tell you if the lamp is on. Two different rooms can have the same list. The rooms differ in how the things stand to one another. Those are facts.

A full description of the world has two parts. The first part is all the facts. The second part is the information that these are all. With both parts you also know everything that is not the case. If the list is complete and “the lamp is on” is not in it, then the lamp is off.

Think of every way that things could be. Wittgenstein calls this field of possibilities logical space. The actual world is one selection from it: the possibilities that are real.

This is the central image of the guide. The dark field around you stands for logical space. A lit point stands for a fact.

Facts come apart cleanly. You can change one and leave all the others as they are. At the next station Wittgenstein limits this claim to the simplest facts (2.061). Try it with three of them.

Why does the book begin with the world, and not with language? Many readers think that the order of discovery was the opposite. Wittgenstein first asked how a sentence can say something. Then he asked what the world must be like so that sentences are possible. On this reading, the first pages are a result and not a foundation. The text does not tell you which way to read it.

What you have now

  • The world is what is the case.
  • It consists of facts, not of things.
  • Facts have a place in a space of possibilities.
  • A complete list of facts also fixes what is not the case.

Station 2 · Remarks 2 – 2.225

2

Proposition 2 goes down to the smallest parts of the world. Then it makes the step on which the whole book depends. It says what a picture is.

Atomic facts and objects

Atomic fact is Ogden's word for the German Sachverhalt. The later translation says state of affairs. It means the simplest possible fact: a fact that has no smaller facts as parts. Be careful. Ogden uses “state of affairs” for a different German word, Sachlage, which means any possible situation, simple or complex.

An atomic fact is a number of objects that are joined together. “Object” is also a technical term. It does not mean a table or a star.

An object is simple. It has no parts. A table has parts, so a table is not an object in this sense. Objects are the points where analysis stops. They are the same in every possible world. Only their arrangement changes. Wittgenstein calls the totality of objects the substance of the world.

Nothing additional holds the objects together. There is no glue between two links of a chain. The links hold one another.

Each object brings its possibilities with it. To know an object is to know all the atomic facts in which it could occur. This range of possibilities is the form of the object.

A comparison (it is not Wittgenstein's): you know what a bishop in chess is when you know all the moves that it can make. You do not need to know where it is on the board now.

Wittgenstein gives no example of an object and no example of an atomic fact. This is deliberate. He does not claim that he has found them. He claims that logic requires them.

Why must there be simple objects?

This is one of the hardest steps in the book. The text gives the argument in two lines.

Here is a common way to rebuild it. Take a sentence about a complex thing: “The broom is in the corner.” A broom is a stick that is attached to a brush. If the stick and the brush come apart, there is no broom, and the sentence is about nothing. So the sentence has sense only if another sentence is true: “The stick is attached to the brush.”

Now suppose that everything is complex in this way, without end. Then each sentence has sense only if a second sentence is true, and the second only if a third is true. We could never know that a sentence has sense before we know facts about the world.

But we do understand a sentence before we know if it is true. So the chain must stop. It stops at names for simple objects. A simple object has no parts that can come apart.

The broom is Wittgenstein's own example, from his later book Philosophical Investigations. There he uses it to criticize this argument.

Now the image from Station 1 is exact. The world is all the atomic facts that exist. The list is complete, so it also fixes which atomic facts do not exist. Atomic facts are independent of one another. If you know one, you can conclude nothing about a second.

This is why three atomic facts give eight possible worlds, and why n atomic facts give 2n.

Pictures

Here the book turns from the world to the way we represent it. The German word Bild means picture, and it also means model. In a notebook from 1914 Wittgenstein mentions a court in Paris. There a road accident was shown to the judges with toy cars and dolls.

A model can show an accident because of three things.

One. Each part of the model stands for one thing. This toy stands for that car.

Two. The parts of the model have a definite arrangement. The arrangement says that the things have the same arrangement.

Three. The model and reality have a form in common. Toys can be arranged in space in all the ways in which cars can be arranged in space. This common range of possible arrangements is the form of representation.

A picture is itself a fact: the fact that its parts are arranged in this way. Only a fact can show a fact. A heap of toys in a bag shows nothing.

Sense and truth are two different things. The sense of a picture is the situation that it shows. You find the sense when you look at the picture alone. To know if the picture is true, you must look at the world. No picture is its own proof.

This is the first sign of where the limit will be. Every picture that has sense can be true and can be false. Something that cannot be false is not a picture of the world.

This is the second sign. A picture shows a situation because it has a form in common with the situation. But a picture cannot go outside its own form and make a picture of that form. A map shows the land by spatial relations. The map cannot also show what a spatial relation is. It can only use spatial relations.

The difference between saying and showing starts here. It becomes the main idea of the book.

Not every picture is spatial. A musical score is a picture of sounds, and a score makes no sound. But every picture has a minimum in common with what it shows: logical form. This is the bare possibility of structure. A picture that needs only this minimum is a logical picture.

What you have now

  • The simplest facts are atomic facts. They are combinations of simple objects.
  • Atomic facts are independent of one another.
  • A picture is a fact that is used as a model of another fact.
  • Its sense is what it shows. Its truth depends on the world.
  • A picture cannot represent its own form. It shows it.

Station 3 · Remarks 3 – 3.5

3

A thought is a picture that needs nothing but logical form. This station shows how a thought becomes visible in signs, and how signs can mislead us.

To think is to make logical pictures. This is a claim about structure. It is not a claim about psychology. Wittgenstein does not ask what happens in a mind. He asks what a thought must be so that it can be true or false.

A thought is a picture, and a picture shows a possible situation. So everything that can be thought is possible. There is no “illogical thought”. An illogical picture would be a picture without form, and that is not a picture.

You cannot give the coordinates of a point that does not exist. In the same way, language cannot describe something that “contradicts logic”. Logic is not a fence around thought with forbidden ground behind it. There is no ground behind it.

The sentence as a fact

Two terms. The propositional sign is the sentence that you can see or hear: ink, sound. The proposition is this sign in use, projected onto the world.

The German word is Satz. It is the usual word for “sentence”. In this book it means a sentence that is used to say that something is the case.

A sentence looks like a list of words. It is not a list. In “the book is on the table” it is important which word is in which position. Wittgenstein makes this clear with an experiment in thought: build the sentence from furniture. Then you see that the arrangement does the work.

Remark 3.1432 says the same in symbols. “aRb” can be a sentence for “a has the relation R to b”. The letter R is not a third name between two names. What says that a is related to b is a fact about the signs: “a” is on the left of “R”, and “b” is on the right. A fact is expressed by a fact.

Names

When analysis is complete, a proposition consists of names. A name stands for one simple object. It does not describe the object. It only stands for it.

Keep two words apart. Sense belongs to propositions: it is the situation that the proposition shows. Meaning belongs to names: it is the object that the name stands for. A name alone says nothing. It does its work only in a proposition.

The German Sinn means “sense”, and it can also mean “direction”. An arrow has a direction. A proposition points at a situation, and it can point correctly or incorrectly. A name, like a point, cannot be wrong.

Wittgenstein believes that each proposition has exactly one complete analysis into names. He does not give a complete analysis of any proposition. Later he decided that this demand was a mistake.

Sign and symbol

A sign is what you see or hear. A symbol is a sign together with its logical use. One sign can belong to different symbols. Everyday language does this all the time, and it hides the difference.

This is Wittgenstein's diagnosis. We see one word, and we think that there is one kind of thing. Many problems of philosophy start in this way. The remedy is a notation in which each sign has one kind of use. Frege and Russell started to build such notations. The rest of the book uses one.

The whole space at once

A proposition marks one place in logical space. But the whole space must be there before you can mark a place. If you understand “it rains”, you also understand “it does not rain” and “it rains or it snows”. You do not learn these one after the other. They come with the first, as the whole grid of coordinates comes with one point.

What you have now

  • A thought is a logical picture of a possible situation.
  • A proposition is a thought that is expressed in signs.
  • The propositional sign is a fact. The arrangement of its parts does the work.
  • Names have meaning. Only propositions have sense.
  • The same sign can hide different symbols. A clear notation shows the difference.

Station 4 · Remarks 4 – 4.53

4

A proposition is a picture that is made of words. From this follows what language can do, what philosophy is, and the difference between saying and showing.

Ogden's “significant proposition” is the German sinnvoller Satz: a proposition that has sense.

We speak without knowing how language works, as we walk without knowing which muscles we use. The grammar on the surface of a sentence is like clothing. It is made for everyday purposes. It does not show the form of the thought below it.

Here Ogden writes “senseless” for the German unsinnig. Later translators write nonsensical. This matters, because Wittgenstein has two different words: sinnlos (without sense) and unsinnig (nonsensical). You will see the difference later at this station. In Ogden's English the two are not always distinct. The guide marks each case.

Look at what the claim is. Wittgenstein does not say that the statements of philosophers are false. He says that most of them do not say anything. The words are there, but no picture is made.

Russell had shown that the grammatical form of a sentence can be different from its logical form. His example: “The present King of France is bald.” It looks like a statement about one person. Russell analysed it as three statements together. There is a King of France. There is not more than one. He is bald.

A picture in words

At first this seems wrong. A sentence does not look like the thing that it describes. Wittgenstein's answer: a score does not look like music, and the groove of a record does not look like a score. But a rule takes you from each of them to the others. They have a structure in common. In the same way, a sentence has logical structure in common with the situation that it describes.

Here is the best evidence that sentences are pictures. You understand sentences that you have never heard before. “A green elephant is asleep on the roof of the opera.” Nobody explained this sentence to you. You know the words, and you see how they are arranged. Thus you know what is the case if the sentence is true. To understand a sentence is exactly this.

The two key verbs appear together here. A proposition shows its sense: you can read from it which situation it pictures. And it says that this situation is real. Showing is what the picture does by its structure. Saying is the claim that the world agrees.

Wittgenstein calls this his fundamental thought. Names stand for objects. But words such as “not”, “and”, “or”, “if” do not stand for anything. There is no object “not” in the world. These words have a different function: they operate on complete pictures.

“It does not rain” pictures the same situation as “it rains”. It only reverses the claim. If this is correct, logic is not a science of special logical objects. This thought drives everything at Stations 5 and 6.

What philosophy is

All true propositions together are natural science. So there is no room for a separate set of philosophical truths. Philosophy cannot be a doctrine. It is an activity: to make clear what we say. “Elucidations” are remarks that help someone to see clearly. They are tools. They are not results.

This is the plan of the book in one line. You cannot mark the limit of language by a description of what is on the other side. To describe it, you would have to say it. You mark the limit from the inside: you make fully clear what can be said. The limit shows where the clear region ends.

Saying and showing

This idea started at 2.172. Now it applies to all of language. A proposition can describe any situation. It cannot describe the logical form that it has in common with the situation. For that, it would have to stand outside logical form, and no proposition can do that.

But logical form is not hidden. It is visible in each proposition, in the way the proposition is built. It is shown.

The book gives examples of things that are shown and not said: that “a” is the name of an object; that one proposition follows from another; that there are objects.

Formal concepts: why “There are objects” says nothing

“Book” is a proper concept. Some things are books, and other things are not. So “there are books” says something, and it could be false.

“Object” is different. It is a formal concept. It does not divide things into two groups. In a logical notation, the variable x itself expresses it. That something is an object is shown by the kind of sign that we use for it.

So “there are objects” tries to say what can only be shown. Wittgenstein calls such a sentence a pseudo-proposition. (Ogden's “senseless” here is again unsinnig, nonsensical.)

Now look at the Tractatus itself. “Objects are simple.” “The world is the totality of facts.” These sentences use formal concepts in exactly the way that 4.1272 excludes. Remark 6.54 answers this problem.

How strong is the difference between saying and showing? Readers disagree. For some, what is shown is a kind of truth that we understand but cannot state. For others, “what is shown” is not a hidden content. It is only our practical ability to use signs correctly. The guide leaves this open until the end.

Elementary propositions and their possibilities

The elementary proposition is the counterpart in language of the atomic fact. It is a direct combination of names. It says that one atomic fact exists. Elementary propositions are independent, as atomic facts are: no elementary proposition can contradict another (4.211). Again the book gives no example.

Take two elementary propositions, p and q. Each can be true or false. So there are four combinations. Wittgenstein calls them truth-possibilities. He writes them as the rows of a table (4.31). Each row is one way the world can be. This is logical space again, now written as a table. Such “truth-tables” are in every logic textbook today. Wittgenstein was one of the first to publish them.

This is the decisive definition. Any proposition, simple or complex, is a selection among the rows. It says “yes” to some truth-possibilities and “no” to the others. To understand the proposition is to know which rows it agrees with. Make some.

Two selections are special. A tautology says “yes” to all rows. A contradiction says “no” to all rows. “It rains or it does not rain” is true in all weather. For that reason it tells you nothing about the weather.

Two different words. In 4.461 “without sense” is sinnlos. In 4.4611 “not senseless” is nicht unsinnig: not nonsensical. So the book has three classes of sentence.

With sense sinnvollPictures a possible situation. Can be true, can be false.“It rains.”
Without sense sinnlosBuilt correctly, but pictures nothing, because it is true (or false) in all cases. A limiting case of language, as 0 is of arithmetic.“It rains or it does not rain.”
Nonsensical unsinnigNot a proposition. A sign in it has no meaning in this use.“Socrates is identical.” (5.473)

At the end of proposition 4, Wittgenstein announces that all propositions have one form in common. In words: “Such and such is the case.” Station 6 gives the exact formula. First we need the machine that builds propositions.

What you have now

  • A proposition is a picture. You understand it when you know what is the case if it is true.
  • Logical words do not stand for objects.
  • Philosophy is an activity of clarification, not a set of truths.
  • What can be shown cannot be said.
  • A proposition selects among truth-possibilities. Tautology and contradiction are the two limits.

Station 5 · Remarks 5 – 5.641

5

The technical centre of the book. One operation builds all propositions from elementary ones. Then, without warning, the path arrives at the limit of the world and at the self.

A function gives one output for each input. A truth-function takes truth-values as input and gives a truth-value as output. “p and q” is a truth-function of p and q. If you know whether p is true and whether q is true, you know whether “p and q” is true. Nothing else is relevant.

Proposition 5 is a very strong claim. It says that every proposition is of this kind. The truth of each proposition depends only on which elementary propositions are true. So if you had all elementary propositions, and knew which are true, you would know everything that can be said (4.26).

Is this credible? “All men are mortal” and “Anna believes that it rains” do not look like truth-functions. Wittgenstein treats both cases at this station (5.52, 5.542). Critics have doubted both treatments from the start.

What follows from what

The truth-grounds of a proposition are the rows in which it is true. With this, logical inference becomes simple. p follows from q when every row that makes q true also makes p true. Then p says nothing that q did not say already.

Inference adds no information. No “laws of inference” are necessary to justify it. If the notation is clear, you see the relation in the two propositions themselves. This is one more case of showing.

Elementary propositions are independent. So no elementary proposition follows from another. Wittgenstein draws a hard conclusion from this. There is no logical connection between one event and a different event. What happens tomorrow does not follow from what happens today.

“Causal nexus” here means a necessary bond between events. Wittgenstein does not deny that science finds regular patterns. He denies that the patterns make events necessary. Station 6 says more about this.

Probability

The same table gives a measure of probability. Count the rows in which r is true. Then count in how many of these rows s is also true. The ratio is the probability that r gives to s. If s is true in all of them, the ratio is 1: s follows from r. Logical certainty is the limiting case of probability. The instrument above shows this ratio.

No logical objects

At 4.0312 you saw the fundamental thought: logical words do not stand for things. Here is an argument for it. Suppose that “∼” (not) is the name of an object. Then “∼∼p” speaks about that object two times, and “p” does not speak about it. They would say different things. But they say the same thing: they select the same rows. So “∼” is not a name.

There is a second argument (5.42). The logical signs can be defined by one another in many ways. You can define “if” from “not” and “or”. You can also define “or” from “not” and “if”. True building blocks could not be exchanged in this way.

One operation

Wittgenstein now reduces all logical words to one operation. Its name is N. Give N any number of propositions. The result is true only if all of them are false. With one proposition, N(p) is “not p”. With two, N(p, q) is “neither p nor q”.

In 1913 Henry Sheffer had shown that “neither … nor” is sufficient to define all the other connectives. Wittgenstein's N extends this to any number of propositions at one time. Try to build the sixteen truth-functions of p and q with N alone.

“All” and “some”

For Wittgenstein, “everything is f” is not a new kind of proposition. Let fa, fb, fc … be all propositions of the form fx. Apply N to all of them together. The result says that none of them is true: “nothing is f”. Apply N to this result: “something is f”.

With a finite list this is clear. If there are infinitely many objects, N must take infinitely many propositions at one time. Since the 1970s there has also been a debate on whether N can express sentences that mix “all” and “some”, such as “everyone loves someone” (Fogelin said no; Geach and Soames replied). And in 1936 Church and Turing proved that there is no general mechanical test for logical truth when “all” and “some” are included. Wittgenstein's remark that there are no surprises in logic (6.1251) fits truth-tables better than it fits the whole of logic.

Identity

In Wittgenstein's notation, each object has one name, and different names stand for different objects. Then the sign “=” is not necessary. Identity is not a relation that some objects have and others do not have. It is shown by the use of the same sign.

“Anna believes that it rains”

This is a problem case. In “Anna believes that it rains”, the proposition “it rains” occurs in a larger proposition. But the truth of the whole does not depend on the truth of the part: Anna can believe something false. This seems to contradict proposition 5.

Wittgenstein's answer is short and obscure. A common explanation: Anna's belief is itself a fact, an arrangement of elements in her mind, and this fact pictures the situation. So the sentence correlates one fact with another fact, element by element. No simple “subject” has a relation to a proposition. Remark 5.5421 draws the conclusion that there is no unified soul of the kind that psychology then assumed.

The limit and the self

This is one of the best-known sentences of the book. It is often misread. It does not mean that a person with more words has a larger world. “Language” here means all propositions that can be formed. “My world” means everything that I could say is the case or is not the case. The two have the same limit, because logic bounds both.

We cannot draw the limit from the outside. We cannot say: “The world contains this, but not that”, where “that” is something that logic excludes. To exclude it we would have to describe it. Then it would be thinkable.

Solipsism is the view that only I and my experience exist. Wittgenstein says something subtle about it. The solipsist tries to say: “The world is my world.” As a statement this fails. It has no opposite that we can picture: I cannot picture a world that is not seen from where I am. But the attempt points at something that shows itself. Every description of the world is given in the language that I understand.

Where is this “I”? It is not in the world. Make a list of all the facts. Include all the facts about your body and your psychology. The subject that sees the world is not an item on the list.

Wittgenstein compares this with the eye and the field of sight. You do not see your eye in the field. And nothing in the field tells you that an eye sees it. His drawing shows what the field of sight is not like:

So the self decreases to a point without size. It is a limit, not a part. And then solipsism and realism are the same. If the “I” is not a thing in the world, then nothing remains of “only my world exists” except this: the world exists.

What you have now

  • Every proposition is a truth-function of elementary propositions.
  • Inference is containment of truth-grounds. It adds nothing.
  • There are no logical objects. One operation, N, builds all propositions.
  • The limits of language are the limits of the world.
  • The subject is not a part of the world. It is its limit.

Station 6 · Remarks 6 – 6.54

6

One formula gives the form of everything that can be said. The remainder of the book examines what the formula excludes: logic, mathematics, the laws of nature, value, and the book itself.

The formula looks difficult. It has three parts, and you know all of them already. Select each part.

This is the limit, drawn from the inside, as 4.114 required. Everything that the procedure can reach is a proposition. Nothing else is.

Proposition 6 now examines the kinds of sentence that do not fit this form. Each kind gets a different verdict.

Logic

People had thought that logic is the most general science: a set of deep truths about everything. Wittgenstein's answer is sobering. The truths of logic are tautologies. They are true in all cases because they say nothing. You do not look at the world to check them. You see it in the sign, for example in a truth-table.

But tautologies are not without use. That certain propositions together give a tautology shows how those propositions are related. Logic shows the “scaffolding” of the world.

“Transcendental” means here: logic is not one of the facts in the world. It is a condition for facts and pictures.

Mathematics

An equation such as 2 × 2 = 4 does not describe a fact. It says that one expression can replace another. In this book a number counts how many times an operation is applied (6.021). So mathematics, like logic, says nothing about the world. We use it to go from one proposition about the world to another.

Science and the net

Newtonian mechanics is like a net with one shape of mesh. That the world can be described with this net tells us nothing about the world. Any world can be described with it, if the mesh is sufficiently fine. What tells us something is how simple the description becomes with this net, compared with a different one.

The laws of nature describe regular patterns. They do not make things happen. They do not explain why there are patterns. Wittgenstein thinks that the modern world treats “law of nature” as a final answer, as earlier ages treated God or Fate. The ancients, he says, at least knew that they had come to a stop.

To expect that the future is like the past is not a logical inference. It is a habit. It is a reasonable habit, but nothing guarantees it.

The first crack: colours

“This point is red” and “this point is blue” cannot both be true. Wittgenstein says that the impossibility must be logical, because there is no other kind. But then the two sentences contradict each other. And elementary propositions cannot contradict each other (4.211).

So he concludes that statements about colour are not elementary. A further analysis must make the hidden contradiction visible. He did not give this analysis.

History: in 1929 he tried to give it, in the paper “Some Remarks on Logical Form”. He failed. Then he gave up the independence of elementary propositions. Many see this as the first crack. Through it the whole system later broke.

Value

The book now turns, in a few pages, to the subject that Wittgenstein thought most important. The argument is short. Every proposition describes a possible fact. Every fact is accidental: it could be different. But if something has value in itself, this cannot be an accident. So value is not a fact, and no proposition states it.

Imagine a book that contains the complete description of the world. It would not contain one judgement that something is good or bad.

Do not read this as contempt for ethics. It is the opposite. Wittgenstein takes ethics out of the area of facts, theories and proofs because he thinks that it is higher than they are. “Transcendental” is the word that he used for logic at 6.13. Like logic, ethics is not in the world. It is a condition of the world as a whole.

A good will or a bad will does not change any fact. It changes the world as a whole: the same facts, seen differently. The limits move. The content does not.

“The mystical” here does not mean visions or secret knowledge. Science can answer how the world is: which facts hold. That there is a world is not one more fact. No proposition says it, because a proposition says how things are, and it assumes that there is something.

To see the world sub specie aeterni (“under the aspect of eternity”, a phrase from Spinoza) is to see it from no place in it: as one bounded whole.

The questions of life do not get an answer. They disappear as questions, because a question exists only where an answer can be said (6.5). “Shows itself” is the same verb as in 4.1212.

The ladder

Ogden's “senseless” is again unsinnig: nonsensical. It is not the mild “without sense” of tautologies. Wittgenstein says that his own propositions are nonsense.

This is the most surprising sentence in the book. Look back. “The world is the totality of facts.” “Objects are simple.” “A proposition is a picture.” Not one of these is a picture of a possible fact that could also be false. Not one is built from elementary propositions with N. By the measure of the book itself, they say nothing.

The book is not a description of the limit. It is a ladder to a place from which you see the limit. Then the ladder must go.

How can nonsense help someone to see? That question divides readers of the Tractatus to this day. After the last station you find their answers. First, take the last step.

Looking back

From here you see the whole structure. The text below is history and interpretation. None of it is Wittgenstein's text.

Three ways to read the ladder

The book calls its own sentences nonsense. Readers have understood this in three main ways.

There are truths that cannot be said

The older reading. For example Elizabeth Anscombe, Peter Hacker.

The sentences of the book are nonsense by its own strict measure. But they are helpful nonsense. They direct us to things that are real and can only be shown: logical form, the existence of objects, value. When the ladder is gone, we keep the insight. We stop the attempt to state it.

The difficulty. “A truth that cannot be said” sounds like the thing that the book excludes. Frank Ramsey put it this way: what we can't say we can't say, and we can't whistle it either.

Nonsense is only nonsense

The “resolute” reading. For example Cora Diamond, James Conant.

There are no truths that cannot be said. The sentences of the book only appear to have sense. The reader first feels that they have sense, and then sees that this feeling was an illusion. The book is a treatment for the desire to do metaphysics. No doctrine remains at the end. Only clarity remains.

The difficulty. The logical results of the book, such as truth-tables and the account of tautology, look like real results. Wittgenstein continued to defend some of them. And it is hard to explain how plain nonsense can have such a careful structure.

A weapon against metaphysics

The Vienna Circle in the 1920s. For example Moritz Schlick, Rudolf Carnap.

Only science says something. Metaphysics is without meaning. The last pages on ethics and the mystical are a personal addition, and you can ignore them.

The difficulty. Wittgenstein rejected this. In 1919 he wrote to the publisher Ludwig von Ficker that the point of the book is an ethical one. He said that the work has two parts: the part that he wrote, and everything that he did not write. The second part, he said, is the important one.

The guide does not choose. You have read the text. The decision is yours.

What Wittgenstein did next

He thought that the problems were solved. He gave away the fortune that he had inherited. He worked as a teacher in village schools, as a gardener in a monastery, and as an architect. In 1929 he returned to Cambridge and to philosophy.

In the years after that he took the Tractatus apart. In the preface to his second book, Philosophical Investigations (1953), he speaks of “grave mistakes” in the first. He gave up these ideas:

  • Independent elementary propositions. The problem of colours showed that they do not work (6.3751).
  • One form for all language. Later he described many different “language-games”: to give orders, to tell a story, to pray, to make a joke. They have no single essence.
  • Meaning as an object. Later he said: in most cases, the meaning of a word is its use in the language.
  • Final analysis. Later he saw no reason to believe that sentences have one hidden, complete analysis into simple parts.

He kept these: philosophy is an activity and not a theory. Its problems come from a wrong view of how our language works. Its aim is clarity.

What stayed in use

  • Truth-tables. Every logic textbook has them. The design of digital circuits uses them.
  • Logical truth as tautology. For the logic of “and”, “or”, “not”, this account is standard.
  • Logical space. The idea of a space of possible worlds became a basic tool of later logic and of the theory of meaning.
  • Saying and showing. The difference is still discussed, in philosophy and outside it.
