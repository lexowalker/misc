# "I'll Never Love Again": lyric inspiration

This is reference material for writing our own lyrics. It names the specific
passage in each source and says what to take from it: a **device**, an
**image** or an **emotional move**. It doesn't give lines to reuse.

Quoting policy: older public-domain sources (St John of the Cross, Tolstoy in
the Garnett translation) are quoted at more length. Copyrighted works (the three
songs, Neruda, Márquez) are named and described, with at most a few words quoted
so you can find the passage. Never put their lines into our lyric.

---

## The central idea everything feeds

**The title is a vow that the song keeps disproving.** "I'll never love again"
is true only because the singer never stopped loving the first time. Several of
the sources below make this exact move. That's why they belong together.

---

## 1. Gary Moore, "Still Got the Blues" (1990)

- **Passage:** the opening verse, about how giving your heart away *used to be*
  easy until you learned there is a *price* for it, and the refrain, where the
  affair was *so long ago* and yet the singer *still* has the blues for her.
- **Device:** **then vs. now.** A plain, conversational past tense ("used to")
  set against an unhealed present ("still"). The whole song sits in the gap
  between those two words.
- **Take:** directness. Moore never reaches for metaphor; the guitar does the
  poetry. Our verses can be equally plain and let the solo carry the excess.
- **Tone:** wistful more than bitter. The love is remembered, not accused.

## 2. Cat Stevens, "The First Cut Is the Deepest" (1967)

- **Passage:** the verse where the singer would have given a new lover *all* of
  his heart, but someone before has *torn it apart* and taken everything, and the
  chorus offer, *"I'll try to love again"*, immediately qualified by the
  title line.
- **Device:** the **exact inverse of our title.** He says "I'll try to love
  again" and we say "I'll never love again." Both mean the same thing: the first
  wound still governs everything. Worth knowing so we don't land too close to it.
- **Take:** the **third person in the room.** The song is sung to someone new
  about someone old. A verse sung to a new, kind person who deserves better,
  and can't have it, is a strong, underused angle.

## 3. Mika, "Happy Ending" (2007)

- **Passage:** the chorus that refuses comfort (*no hope, no love, no glory*,
  no happy ending), and the line about loving *like it's forever*, then living
  the rest of your lives *but not together*.
- **Device:** **refusal to pretend.** The singer rejects the consoling story.
  Also: forever-in-the-moment against the long, ordinary afterwards.
- **Take:** the **flat ending.** Not a fight, not a betrayal, just a love that
  stopped. Musically the song builds to a big choral climax over that bleak
  statement. The contrast between swelling sound and hopeless words maps neatly
  onto our solo crescendo.

## 4. Pablo Neruda

### *Twenty Love Poems and a Song of Despair* (1924), Poem 20, "Puedo escribir los versos más tristes esta noche"
This is the single most relevant text in the list.
- **Passages to read:**
  - The refrain: he *can write the saddest lines tonight*.
  - The starry night, which is *more immense without her*.
  - The famous line *"Es tan corto el amor, y es tan largo el olvido"* (love is
    so short, forgetting is so long).
  - The self-contradiction: *"Ya no la quiero, es cierto"* (I don't love her
    anymore, that's certain), first followed by *how much I loved her*, and later
    by *but maybe I love her*.
  - The jealous fragment: she'll belong *to another*, as she did *before my
    kisses*.
  - The ending: this may be the *last pain* she causes him and these the *last
    lines* he writes her. That is a vow of ending, made inside the poem that
    proves he can't stop.
- **Device:** the **declaration that undoes itself.** This is our title's engine.
- **Take:** the poem's **timeline asymmetry** (short love, long forgetting) and
  its **setting**: one night, one sky, the same trees whitening the same as
  before, while the people are no longer the same.

### "La canción desesperada" (the Song of Despair, same book)
- **Passage:** the recurring *"Es la hora de partir"* (it is the hour of leaving),
  and the image of being abandoned *like the wharves at dawn*.
- **Take:** **place-as-feeling.** An empty dock, a departure hour. Concrete
  settings that hold the emotion so the lyric doesn't have to name it.

### *One Hundred Love Sonnets* (1959), Sonnet XVII
- **Passage:** loving someone *as certain dark things are loved*, secretly,
  *between the shadow and the soul*, and loving without knowing how, when or
  from where.
- **Take:** love as **something below explanation.** Useful for a verse that
  admits the singer can't say why it's still her.

### "Si tú me olvidas" (If You Forget Me)
- **Passage:** the conditional bargain: if you stop loving me *little by little*,
  I'll stop loving you little by little; if you forget me suddenly, don't look
  for me.
- **Take:** the **bargain the heart can't keep.** Contrast with our singer, who
  has been forgotten and *can't* reciprocate the forgetting.

## 5. Leo Tolstoy

### *Anna Karenina* (1877)
- **Kitty refuses Levin (Part 1).** A proposal turned down. Levin decides his
  life of love is over. **Irony for us:** he's wrong. Later (Part 4) he and Kitty
  understand each other by chalking only the *initial letters* of words on a card
  table. The "never again" vow broken. Keep it in mind as the shadow under our
  title: listeners know vows like this are rarely kept.
- **Karenin at Anna's sickbed (Part 4).** After childbirth fever, the cold
  husband forgives her completely and weeps. **Take:** grace arriving too late
  to save anything.
- **Anna's last journey (Part 7, ch. 29–31).** The famous candle (Garnett):
  > "...and the candle by which she had read the book filled with trouble,
  > falsehood, sorrow, and evil, flared up more brightly than ever before,
  > lighted up for her all that had been in darkness, flickered, began to grow
  > dim, and was quenched forever."
  **Take:** the final brightening before the dark, which is the shape of the solo's
  climax and resolution.
- **Device throughout:** the **small domestic detail** that wounds more than
  the big scene: a gesture, an object, an ear (Anna suddenly noticing her
  husband's ears). Use one such detail per verse.

### *War and Peace* (1869)
- **Prince Andrei and the oak (Vol. II, Part 3, ch. 1 and 3).** He passes a huge,
  bare, scarred old oak that seems to refuse spring, and agrees with it: life is
  over for him. Weeks later, after first seeing Natasha, he passes it again and
  it's covered in new leaves. **Take:** a single recurring image that changes
  between verse 1 and the last verse. This is the strongest structural gift in
  the list.
- **Andrei's deathbed (Vol. IV, Part 1, ch. 16).** Dying, he realizes love is
  God, that to love is to be part of it, and his love for Natasha becomes
  something larger and less personal. **Take:** a bridge where personal loss
  opens into something almost sacred. This connects directly to St John below.
- **Natasha after the betrayal with Anatole (Vol. II, Part 5).** Shame and grief
  at having broken something good herself. **Take:** an option for the singer
  to be the one at fault.

### *Family Happiness* (1859, novella)
- **Passage:** the ending, where the narrator's passionate first love with her
  husband has burned out and become a quieter, different love.
- **Take:** a mature counter-voice: the love doesn't die, it changes shape.
  Useful if the final chorus should feel resigned rather than wrecked.

## 6. Saint John of the Cross (16th c.)

Public domain. Spanish originals with my own literal translations.

### "Cántico espiritual" (Spiritual Canticle), stanza 1
This is the opening of the loved-and-lost tradition in Spanish:
> ¿Adónde te escondiste,
> Amado, y me dejaste con gemido?
> Como el ciervo huiste,
> habiéndome herido;
> salí tras ti clamando, y eras ido.

*Where have you hidden, Beloved, and left me moaning? / You fled like the stag,
having wounded me; / I went out after you, calling, and you were gone.*

- **Take:** **the wound and the flight in one breath**, plus the image of going
  out calling into emptiness. The final *"y eras ido"* (and you were gone) is a
  perfect model of a short, devastating last line.

### "Noche oscura" (The Dark Night), stanzas 1 and 8
> En una noche oscura,
> con ansias, en amores inflamada,
> ¡oh dichosa ventura!,
> salí sin ser notada,
> estando ya mi casa sosegada.

*On a dark night, / burning with love's longing, / oh happy chance! / I went
out unseen, / my house being now at rest.*

> Quedéme y olvidéme,
> el rostro recliné sobre el Amado;
> cesó todo, y dejéme,
> dejando mi cuidado
> entre las azucenas olvidado.

*I stayed, and forgot myself, / my face laid upon the Beloved; / all ceased, and
I let myself go, / leaving my cares / forgotten among the lilies.*

- **Take:** **the dark night as passage, not just suffering.** Darkness is where
  the lover is guided only by *the light that burned in the heart* (stanza 3).
  It's a model for a bridge where the singer stops resisting the grief.

### "Llama de amor viva" (Living Flame of Love), stanzas 1–2
> ¡Oh llama de amor viva,
> que tiernamente hieres
> de mi alma en el más profundo centro! ...
> ¡Oh cauterio suave!
> ¡Oh regalada llaga!

*O living flame of love / that tenderly wounds / my soul in its deepest
centre! ... / O gentle cautery! / O delightful wound!*

- **Take:** the **paradox of the sweet wound**: the singer would not give up the
  pain because it is the last of the love. That gives a reason *why* "I'll never
  love again" is chosen, not just suffered.

## 7. Gabriel García Márquez

### *Love in the Time of Cholera* (1985)
- **Opening line:** the scent of *bitter almonds* always reminding the doctor of
  the fate of *unrequited love*. **Take:** a **sense memory** tied to loss (a
  smell, a song, a street).
- **Florentino's lovesickness (Part 2).** His symptoms are mistaken for cholera
  by his mother. **Take:** love as illness, with diagnosis, fever, no cure.
- **Fermina in the Arcade of the Scribes (Part 2).** She sees him up close in the
  market, realizes it was *an illusion*, and ends it with one gesture. **Take:**
  how small the instant of ending can be compared with a lifetime of aftermath.
- **The waiting.** Florentino waits *fifty-one years, nine months and four days*.
  Meanwhile he has hundreds of affairs while keeping his heart "untouched" for
  her. **Take:** **the body moves on, the heart doesn't.** It's a sharp, morally
  complicated reading of "I'll never love again."
- **Ending (Part 6).** The riverboat flying the cholera flag. Asked how long they
  can keep going up and down the river, Florentino answers: *"Toda la vida"*
  (forever, or all our lives). **Take:** the counterweight to our title: the
  same absolute vow, made in hope instead of loss.

### *One Hundred Years of Solitude* (1967)
- **Amaranta.** She refuses Pietro Crespi, who then kills himself. She burns her
  hand in the stove and wears a *black bandage* until her death, and spends her
  last years weaving her own shroud. **Take:** **the self-imposed sentence**, the
  closest literary twin to our title as a lifelong vow kept out of grief and pride.
- **Rebeca eating earth** from longing. **Take:** grief as a physical hunger.
- **Mauricio Babilonia's yellow butterflies,** which follow him everywhere and
  announce him to Meme. **Take:** a magical-realist image for a love that keeps
  arriving even after the person is gone.
- **Final line:** races condemned to solitude get no *second opportunity on
  earth*. **Take:** the finality of the title, stated on a cosmic scale.

## Map: where each source can serve the song

| Song section | Job | Strongest sources |
|---|---|---|
| Verse 1 | Concrete scene, *then* vs. *now*, one recurring image introduced | Moore ("used to / still"), Tolstoy's oak, Márquez's bitter almonds, Neruda's night sky |
| Verse 2 | Sung to someone new, or the small detail that wrecks you | Cat Stevens (new lover), Tolstoy domestic detail, Florentino (body vs. heart) |
| Chorus | The vow, and its quiet self-contradiction | Neruda Poem 20 ("ya no la quiero... tal vez la quiero"), Mika (no happy ending) |
| Bridge (before solo) | Grief turning into something larger, the sweet wound | St John (Living Flame, Dark Night), Andrei's deathbed |
| Solo | Wordless: the flight, the calling out, the brightening before the dark | St John's *salí tras ti clamando* (I went out after you, calling), Anna's candle |
| Final chorus | The title means something new: chosen fidelity, not defeat | Amaranta, Florentino's *toda la vida* inverted, Neruda's "last lines" |
| Last line | Short, absolute, quiet | *"y eras ido"* (and you were gone) as the model |

## Tensions worth exploiting

- **Short love / long forgetting** (Neruda)
- **Vow vs. evidence:** saying "never" while every line shows still-loving (Neruda, Levin)
- **Wound you'd refuse to heal** (St John's sweet wound)
- **Body moves on, heart stays** (Florentino)
- **Swelling music, hopeless words** (Mika), which is also the solo's job
- **Recurring image transformed** (Tolstoy's oak)

## Guardrails

- Our own lines only. Read these for moves and images, then close the book.
- Avoid the reference titles as phrases inside our lyric ("first cut",
  "happy ending", "still got the blues") unless it's a deliberate, small nod.
- One big image per section. These sources are dense; the song shouldn't be.
